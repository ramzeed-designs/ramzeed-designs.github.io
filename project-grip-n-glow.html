01The challenge

Holding things gently is hard work.

What is cerebral palsy?

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a group of conditions caused by damage to the developing brain, usually before or around birth. It affects movement, muscle tone and posture. It lasts for life, but regular therapy helps children build strength and control.

For our Design for Special Needs module, we started by spending time with the people we were designing for. We visited the CP unit at Health and Care Foundation Hospital and the Blind People's Association in Ahmedabad, and went into the homes of children with special needs to understand their daily lives beyond the clinic. Across these visits, one everyday struggle stood out for children with CP: holding things with the right amount of force. A cup is squeezed too hard, or slips away. That control depends on three things CP affects:

  • 1Motor skills
  • 2Muscle tone
  • 3Sensory feedback

This leads to frustration, less independence, and dropped or broken things, or even getting hurt.

The exercise

Squeeze, hold, let go. Again.

Physiotherapists build grip with simple squeeze exercises, repeated many times in every session. Each squeeze strengthens the hand muscles and teaches the brain how much force to use. It works, but it is repetitive, and a child can't see how hard they are squeezing.

  1. 1Squeeze
  2. 2Hold
  3. 3Let go
  4. ↻ repeat

Grip exercises help, but they are repetitive and there are few playful tools for grip control. Activities that mix something to see, something to feel and lots of repetition are known to help motor coordination, so we gave every squeeze a light to chase.

02How it works

Squeeze, and the light climbs.

  1. 1

    Squeeze the bulb

    The child grips a rubber squeezer. It takes real effort, so each squeeze is an exercise.

  2. 2

    The sensor feels it

    An air tube carries the pressure to a sensor read by an Arduino Uno.

  3. 3

    Light climbs to the top

    The harder the grip, the higher the LEDs climb. The goal: make it reach the top.

  • Arduino Uno
  • Air-pressure sensor
  • LED strip
  • Squeeze bulb + air tube
  • Wooden housing

04Expert feedback

What the physiotherapists said.

We took the working prototype to a therapy centre. The physiotherapists tried it first, and our mentor's review was just as positive.

What worked

  • Therapists can see how much pressure the child is applying
  • The lights make the exercise less intimidating
  • Fun for kids, so they keep going

What they asked for

  • A softer bulb, like a BP bulb with a valve
  • A force sensor for hand and leg force too
  • Different kinds of grips
05User testing

Testing with children.

With their therapists guiding them, we tried the prototype with two children who both need to build grip strength.

Boy, 5 years

Hemiplegic CP

Hemiplegia is the most common type of CP. It affects one side of the body: muscle weakness or stiffness, trouble grasping objects, poor fine motor skills, and muscles that stay clenched (spasticity).

  • Right side affected
  • Grip strength needed
Toddler, 2 years

Developmental delay

A child who reaches movement milestones later than expected. Grip work at this age helps the hands catch up for everyday things like holding a cup or a crayon.

  • Grip strength needed

These sessions showed us it isn't only for CP: anyone building grip strength or learning to judge force can use it.

06Where it stopped

A next version, on paper.

Using the feedback, we planned a next version: one device that works for more than grip, with a force sensor so therapists can use it for other hand and leg exercises too, and more of a game, with levels and a way to track each child's progress from session to session. The course ended before we built it and we didn't take it further, but the therapists' response showed the idea works.
