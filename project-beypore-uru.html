02Why the Uru

It started with a dhow in Qatar.

On a visit to Qatar I saw dhows displayed proudly as part of the country's maritime heritage. Only later did I learn that many of them are built back home, in the small coastal town of Beypore, Kerala. That mix of curiosity and pride is where this book began.

Beypore's Urus still sail for Gulf royal families. One, stitched together with coir rope and no nails, was built for the FIFA World Cup 2022 in Qatar.

"Uru" means "fat" in Malayalam, for its wide, deep hull

03Why Beypore

Where the river meets the sea.

Beypore sits where the Chaliyar river meets the Arabian Sea. The calm estuary was ideal for building and launching big ships.

  • Timber by riverTeak from the Nilambur forests was tied into bamboo rafts, called therappam, and floated 68 km downstream.
  • The monsoonRegular winds carried Arab traders to the Malabar coast and back, and they brought dhow-building ideas with them.
  • Royal patronageThe Zamorins of Calicut gave timber rights and ordered ships, turning a local skill into a whole economy.
04The place

A shipyard you can only reach by boat.

Today the Urus are built on a small island in the estuary, hidden by coconut palms. Nothing of the work can be seen from the mainland. A boatman ferries the workers over at 5 a.m. and brings them back at 5:30 p.m.

Getting there was the hardest part. The fishermen's boats couldn't make the trip. Then someone pointed out the workers' ferry, already leaving, while I was still on my scooter on a swaying barge. I chased it down and talked the boatman into taking me across.

05The people

The plans live in people, not on paper.

No drawings, no blueprints. Shipbuilding knowledge in Beypore has always been passed on by watching and doing, usually from father to son. Two groups share the work.

Odayis · Mesthiris

The master carpenters. They read the buyer's model, work out the timber and the proportions in their heads, and guide every cut.

Khalasis

The movers and finishers. They bring timber to the hull, seal it with fish oil and cotton, and launch the ship with pulleys, rollers and songs.

06Materials & tools

Teak, coir, fish oil and a chisel.

  • TeakFrom Nilambur. Keel, hull and deck, because it shrugs off seawater.
  • Coir ropeCoconut fibre that binds planks and packs the seams.
  • Fish oil & resinBrushed between planks to keep the water out.
  • UliChisels of every size, for cutting and carving joints.
  • KundakolliThe wooden mallet that drives the chisel.
  • Hand sawBig logs are still cut to size by hand, two men to a saw.

    09What's at stake

    A rare craft, without a pension.

    The skill is world-famous, but the people who hold it are not well looked after.

    • Teak keeps getting more expensive, so fewer ships are ordered.
    • Young people are choosing lighter, steadier work.
    • Wages aren't standardised, and middlemen take an unseen share.
    • Senior craftsmen worked as daily-wage labour, so they retire with no pension.
    • Machine-built ships keep shrinking the market.

    Some craftsmen now make miniature Urus for visitors. Through this book I wanted more people to see the hidden world of the Uru-makers, while it's still here.
